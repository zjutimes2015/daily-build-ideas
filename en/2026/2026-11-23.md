# Daily Build Idea Brief · 2026-11-23

**In one line:** A study just proved your "zero data retention" checkbox only binds the first hop — build a service that plants canary tokens in prompts, so you can see exactly who reads them and through which route, and grab the first-two-weeks window of the "AI supply chain leak" story that just blew up.

## Today's Build Idea: PromptCanary — "Plant canaries in your prompts, see who steals them"

**Product:** For teams calling models through AI gateways / aggregators (OpenRouter, Vercel AI Gateway, etc.): plant unique canary secrets (fake AWS keys, unique URLs, DNS names) inside prompts, agent context, and API payloads. The moment anyone uses one where it should never appear, you get an alert — pinpointing which route and which provider leaked it.

**Who it's for:** Small teams feeding code, customer data, and internal docs to AI; developers saving money on cheap aggregators without knowing where their prompts end up; small companies with strict compliance needs that still have to use third-party models.

**Core features:**

1. One-click seeding: automatically insert canaries (fake keys, unique URLs, DNS records) into prompts or agent context, without affecting model output.
2. Leak listening: a full honeypot stack — DNS, HTTP, AWS, Git, SSH — that fires the moment a key gets tried.
3. Attribution report: which route, which provider, which call leaked it — "the leak follows the seller, not the platform," and the report names names.
4. Alerts that reach you: Slack / email / webhook within hours of a key being tried — not when the bill arrives.
5. Route health checks: regularly probe the routes you actually use with canaries, and tell you which ones are clean and which ones are reading your prompts.

**How it charges:** Free tier — 10 active canaries, 1 project, email alerts; $19/mo Pro (unlimited canaries, Slack alerts, attribution reports); $49/mo Team (multi-seat, API, SIEM integration). Cost math: the listening side is a few honeypot servers at a few dollars a month — you're selling "knowing who stole your prompt," not compute.

**Why it's a small-team opportunity:** Big companies have in-house security teams that will slowly build this; small teams are running naked today — every dollar they save on aggregators can come back as a leak. And detection needs no big model, just honeypots + a dashboard: one person can build it in weeks.

**Honest boundaries:** Classic canaries already exist (Thinkst Canarytokens, free) — the gap is "prompt-specific": attribution by route and by call, and seeding designed for LLM payloads; detection only finds leaks after the fact, it can't prevent them — the product sells "knowing," not "stopping"; attribution depends on canary design — a careful reader who only reads and never touches a key stays invisible, so coverage is not 100%; this is a different product in the same security lane as Nov 19's PatchCheck (vulnerability exposure scanning vs prompt-leak detection) — don't build the same thing twice.

## Why now

- **On 2026-11-19, Dragos' HD Moore (author of Metasploit) published a five-month study.** From July to November 2026, his team sent roughly two million prompts to widely used AI services, each carrying a unique canary secret (real AWS keys, SSH passwords, unique URLs / DNS names, private git references), then watched who used them.
- **At least 3 AI providers had already been breached.** In one late-October window, a couple hundred bait AWS keys were all stolen — including requests the provider had rejected before any model ran; the stolen keys triggered tens of thousands of AWS API calls, tried within hours, listing S3 buckets, enabling Bedrock, adding IAM users.
- **"Zero data retention" only binds the first hop.** A Chinese AI lab's systems fetched the secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models — including roughly two-thirds of requests explicitly marked zero data retention / no data collection; the same model resold through US providers triggered the identical scraper.
- **Resellers need no exploit at all to read your prompts.** In inference marketplaces, the cheapest seller just turns on request logging and reads everything — one person opened secret URLs from a home broadband connection and guessed usernames by hand; the same model through a different seller was never touched. The leak follows the seller.
- **The bill is already in.** Railway estimates stolen customer keys drove about $740,000 in unauthorized AI usage during the October wave, touching up to 100,000 workspaces at most (an upper-bound estimate); Wiz counts at least 9 AI developer platforms disclosing similar incidents in October — "Black October."
- **The coverage hands you the product definition.** Dragos' own advice includes: "seed your own canaries into agent contexts and treat any use as an incident." That's the product in one sentence.
- **Regulatory tailwind:** The FTC said as early as January 2024 that companies hosting models for others may break the law if they mislead customers about how their data is collected.
