# Daily Build Idea Brief · 2026-11-27

**One-line summary:** 1Password disclosed a backend breach that downloaded customer names and emails (vaults unaffected) — build a "blast radius translator" that tells your team, when a vendor you use gets breached, whether you need to act and exactly what to do, before everyone has to read the full disclosure themselves.

## Today's Build Idea: BlastCheck — "Your vendor got breached. Here's what your team does."

**Product:** An action-translation service for third-party vendor security incidents. You add the SaaS your team uses (password manager, CI, cloud, payments, email) to a watchlist; when any of them discloses a security incident, BlastCheck immediately turns the official disclosure into an action list for your team: are we affected, what exactly leaked, what credentials to rotate, what phishing to expect next, and who confirms it's done — instead of making you read a long post written for legal and PR.

**Who it's for:** Small teams and indie developers with no dedicated security hire (the founder doubling as CISO, first to be asked "do we need to change our passwords?"); remote teams running on 1Password / GitHub / assorted SaaS; IT consultants managing vendors for multiple clients (one panel for every client's vendor incidents).

**Core features:**

1. Vendor watchlist: pick from a directory of common SaaS or paste a domain to auto-detect; the list means you only hear about incidents that concern you, not the whole internet's CVE noise.
2. Incident translation: pull the vendor's official disclosure and status page into a fixed format — data affected / data not affected / attack path / what the vendor already did / what you need to do — every line linked back to the source.
3. Action checklist: break "what you need to do" into checkable tasks (rotate this class of token, review invites and recovery emails from this window, warn everyone about phishing impersonating this vendor), assign them, track completion.
4. Phishing watch: the 30 days after a disclosure are peak season for impersonation phishing; BlastCheck generates concrete "here's the fake email you may receive" warnings per incident type and posts them to your team channel.
5. Incident timeline: one thread per vendor — disclosure, follow-ups, technical blog, closure — so the next incident can be compared against "what they said last time."

**Pricing:** Free tier watches 3 vendors with email alerts; $19/month Pro (unlimited vendors, action checklists, phishing watch, Slack notifications); $49/month Team (multi-client panels, task assignment and completion audit, incident report export). Cost math: fetching and summarizing are lightweight; you're selling "the hours your whole team doesn't spend reading disclosures and guessing," not compute.

**Why it's a small-team opportunity:** Enterprise vendor-risk tools sell to companies with compliance departments — heavy process, high prices, and they only cover pre-contract assessment, not "what do we do tonight." The small-team reality is different: the disclosure explodes to 1,600+ points on HN, the boss asks in chat "we use this — are we okay?", and nobody can give a sourced answer in a minute. BlastCheck does exactly one thing: turns disclosures into checklists. Once the vendor list and incident archive accumulate, "check BlastCheck first" becomes the default move.

**Honest boundaries:** This is information translation, not a security audit — it can't judge risks beyond the disclosure, and it can't guarantee the vendor told the whole truth (1Password itself says the technical blog comes after the investigation). V1 must stay strictly on "official disclosure → action list," with every claim linked to source text and no security judgments of its own. Monetization is also unproven: whether small teams pay monthly for a tool used a few times a year is an open question — per-incident pricing or bundling with credential management may fit better. Measure real open rates on the free tier before committing to a pricing shape.

## Why Now

- **From 2026-11-19, 1Password investigated a backend intrusion.** An attacker gained access to an employee's corporate GitHub account, pushed malicious code to backend services, and downloaded account records for a subset of customers — names, emails, account identifiers, sign-in times, plus a small number of invitation/provisioning/recovery email tokens. Vault contents and the keys protecting them were not affected (per the official disclosure).
- **On 2026-11-25, 1Password disclosed publicly.** The post is thorough, but it's written for everyone: whether your team needs to act, and how, is scattered between "what we've done" and "what you can do" — you have to assemble it yourself.
- **The developer community's reaction today is the #2 story site-wide:** Hacker News #2 today, 1,630 points / 702 comments (verified 08:30 CST); Lobsters same topic at 45 points / 4 comments. Much of the 702-comment thread is "we use this — now what?" — exactly the sentence BlastCheck exists to answer.
- **The gap is real:** status pages say "all systems operational," news says "a breach happened," and nobody is responsible for translating the disclosure into your team's todo list for tonight. The attack path (employee GitHub account → CI/CD → backend) is precisely the setup most small teams run — every team wants to know whether the same thing could happen at their other vendors.
- **Precedent:** every time professional information overload appears, someone wins with the translation layer — CVE disclosures got vulnerability databases, outages got status aggregators. Vendor security incidents still have no default "action translation" answer for small teams. The window is now: the incident is on HN's front page and team anxiety is real.
